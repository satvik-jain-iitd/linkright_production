### Consolidated Data Layer (CDL) Strategy -- Current Project (PI 26.1 -- Present)

Satvik's most recent and current project at Acme Bank is the Consolidated Data Layer (CDL) strategy, which emerged from the second strategic pivot. The core problem is this: over the past year, a data platform team built a Data Access Manager (DAM) layer as a unified data source for GCIP (Global Compliance Intelligence Platform). While the DAM was an important foundational step, it delivers only fractionally standardized values -- a significant portion of data remains in raw, unstandardized SOR (System of Record) formats. The CRR product cannot use this data for accurate risk scoring because rules require consistently standardized and categorized values to function correctly. This data gap became the key dependency blocking CRR modernization.

To illustrate the scale: for Product Type alone, there are 136,738 raw unstandardized source records that need to be compressed to 12,408 standardized records and then to just 64 business-readable categories -- an 11:1 compression ratio from source to standardized, and a 2,137:1 compression from source to categories. For Industry Type, the numbers are even larger: 521,583 raw records compressing to 42,182 standardized and just 98 categories (12.3:1 and 5,322:1 compression ratios respectively). Currently, the cross-reference and categorization mappings are maintained fully manually by the Cadence business team via Excel uploads -- painful, error-prone, and unscalable.

Satvik built the CDL strategy and execution plan. The CDL defines a 5-step data pipeline: Translation (mapping raw SOR values to standard codes), Standardization (normalizing across sources), Categorization (grouping into business-readable categories), Derivation (computing derived data points from standardized inputs), and Master List (maintaining authoritative reference data). The scope covers 54 data points across Individual and Entity/Intermediary products. Of these, 8 require no standardization (they are already clean), 21 are in the P1+P2 priority group covering 58% of risk elements (67 out of 115), 17 are in P3+P4 covering 96.5% of risk elements (111 out of 115), and 8 are in a backlog group pending business confirmation. The strategy aligns with BRD requirements 12.16 (Rescoring Capabilities), 12.17 (Data Considerations -- specifically the data mapping resolution mandate), 12.18 (Performance Reporting), and 12.19 (AI/ML Based Scoring, which requires clean standardized training data as a prerequisite).

The execution roadmap spans PI 26.2 through 27.5, with Phase 0 (pre-standardization and discovery) in PI 26.2-26.3, Phase 1 (Individual portfolio -- P1 through P4 data points) in PI 26.3-27.1, Phase 2 (Entity/Intermediary portfolio) in PI 26.4-27.5, and continuous data governance running throughout. The strategy also includes an AI/ML POC for automated standardization and a Master List implementation for centralized, governed reference data. Satvik currently works on this project with a small team of 4 members with direct visibility to leadership.

My friends thought I was being reckless just by entertaining the idea. And the longer I sat with it, the more I realized something uncomfortable: I didn't actually want to go back into a classroom. What I wanted was to build something. The classroom, even a progressive one, was still a classroom. So I said no to Masters Union and yes to a blank canvas, and that decision is what became LinkRight.

The original idea was modest. I had been solving my own job search problem at the time — applying to PM roles, getting filtered out, trying to make my resume communicate the actual weight of what I had done at AcmeBank and TechCo SaaS. The frustration was specific: I knew my work was strong, but the way it was documented was flattening it. So I started building a resume optimization tool. I called it "Resume ImproverAI" internally, and the plan was simple — upload your resume, get better bullets. But somewhere in the first few weeks of building, I kept running into the same structural problem: the tool didn't know me. Every generation was stateless. It produced reasonable output but had no sense of continuity, no understanding of how my experiences connected to each other, no way to build on what it had learned about me in the previous session. And that's when the idea mutated. The real problem wasn't the resume format — it was that there was no persistent memory of a career. The resume was just a symptom. What was missing was a career graph: a semantic network of experiences, achievements, skills, and outcomes that could be queried intelligently and translated into exactly the right artifact for exactly the right opportunity. That insight changed everything. What started as a resume tool became a five-pillar career navigation operating system: Discover (build your memory layer), Find via Scout (match intelligently against real job openings), Outreach (generate every application artifact in one click), Prepare (personalized interview drills that know your gaps), and Broadcast (draft and schedule authentic LinkedIn content from your real daily wins). The product I was building for myself turned out to be a product I believed every ambitious job-seeker in India deserved to have.

The part I had not planned for was the building itself. I am a product manager by training and by instinct. I know how to define a problem, how to write a PRD, how to build a roadmap, how to run a sprint. I am not a developer. I had never owned a production codebase. I had written SQL at TechCo SaaS, built Python scripts for Walmart data pipelines, done some exploratory scripting — but sitting down with a full-stack production system and understanding it deeply enough to extend it, debug it, and ship to real users was an entirely different thing. I chose to build with Claude Code as my collaborator, which is both the most honest thing I can say about this project and the most interesting. I didn't use it as a code generator that I blindly pasted from. I used it the way I would use a very senior engineer who was infinitely patient — I asked it to explain what every function was doing, I insisted on comments in Romanized Hindi so I could follow the logic in my own language, I pushed back when something felt hacky, and I learned by reading every diff before it went to main. The architecture I ended up building on — Next.js with React 19 on the frontend, FastAPI and Python on the backend, Supabase for the database, Neo4j for the career graph layer, and a Rust CLI called Truss for parallel agent orchestration — is not what a cautious first-time builder would choose. It's what the product actually needed. I chose complexity where complexity was justified and simplicity everywhere else.

The LLM experimentation phase was one of the most grounding experiences of the entire build. Resume parsing — extracting clean, structured, accurate career data from a PDF — sounds deceptively simple until you actually try to do it at production quality. My first instinct was to use local models running on an Oracle Cloud VPS I had set up, primarily because free compute with no rate limits felt like the right foundation for something I was self-funding. I ran benchmarks on every model available through Ollama on the VPS: llama3.2:1b, llama3.2:3b, qwen2.5:1.5b, qwen2.5:3b, gemma3:1b, phi4-mini, and several others. I measured wall time, token throughput, accuracy, and hallucination rate systematically. The results were humbling. The 1.5B parameter models were fast but produced too many errors. The 3B models were more accurate but ran at 180 seconds or more per resume on CPU-only compute — completely impractical for a product where the user is watching a loading screen. I tried map-reduce architectures to parallelize the extraction, and the reduce step collapsed under the combined output load. None of the local models came close to production quality at acceptable speed. The answer turned out to be Gemini 2.5 Flash-Lite: 4 seconds per resume, over 400 tokens per second, and accuracy that matched what I was seeing from larger models. That is a 50x speed improvement over my best local model. The architecture I settled on for the parser was a hybrid — deterministic regex extraction for contact details, education, and skills (zero LLM cost, zero hallucination risk), with the LLM called only for the genuinely hard fields: work experience tree, project structure, certifications, and the first-person career narration. I built in three-key rotation for Gemini so a 429 rate limit on one key would automatically fall through to the next, and added Oracle Ollama and Groq as fallback tiers before Gemini in the production pipeline. That fallback chain — Oracle to Groq to Gemini — meant the product was resilient against any single provider going down.

The hardest lessons were the ones that didn't show up in any specification. The first time I hit a production bug — the resume parse was failing silently for new signups — my instinct was to investigate methodically, form a hypothesis, explain it to myself, and then fix it. What I learned is that in a self-funded solo product with real users, you don't have the luxury of explanation chains. You read the error, you check what you know, and you fix the root cause. Speed matters. Documentation matters less than resolution. There was a moment early in the build where I spent three days building a custom memory orchestration system before I had a single user on the platform — a beautiful, flexible, extensible infrastructure that solved a problem I didn't yet know I had. A friend whose product philosophy I respect a lot calls this pattern "infra as procrastination" — you dress up delay as progress because building systems feels productive even when it isn't shipping product. I had to internalize a three-question test before making any infrastructure decision: Does this directly benefit a real user? Can I ship without it? Am I building infrastructure or product? If the answer to the second question was yes, I learned to skip it. There were smaller lessons too. My Vercel deployments were mysteriously blocking for a week before I discovered the git author in my local config was wrong — it needed to be `satvik-jain-iitd` or Vercel refused to deploy. I burned through all three Gemini API keys in a single testing session by running model comparisons too aggressively and hit 429 errors simultaneously across all keys. I learned that rate limits exist even in testing, and that the minimum gap between API calls is not a suggestion. These were not elegant lessons. They were the kind you remember because they cost you time on a thing you cared about.

What I discovered about myself as a builder is that I am fundamentally a practical learner — the same person who scored 10/10 in every lab at IIT Delhi while struggling in theoretical courses is the same person who built a production-grade resume parser from first principles while learning what an API rate limit actually feels like in practice. I track every task in a CLI tool called Beads before I write a single line of code. I do not start a feature without an issue, and I do not mark an issue closed without testing the result. I dogfood obsessively — every parsing test I ran used either my own resume or a colleague's real resume, never fabricated data. I deployed every meaningful change directly to `sync.linkright.in` and verified it in a real browser rather than assuming the tests told the full story. Of the 57 issues I created across the full project lifecycle, I closed 44 — 77% of everything I planned, I shipped, alone, while holding down a full-time role at Acme Bank and advising GoGoGo on the side. That's not a credential I'm claiming on a resume. It's a description of how I work: high discipline, high ownership, low ceremony, no team to hide behind.

The thing I am most proud of in this project is the conceptual clarity that emerged. The most important realization — and it came late, maybe six months into the build — is that LinkRight is not a resume builder. This sounds like a trivial distinction but it isn't. A resume builder is a formatting tool. It takes your existing experience and makes it prettier. What LinkRight is actually building is a career memory system: a persistent, growing, queryable semantic graph of everything you have done, everything you have learned, and every outcome you have driven. The resume is just one output of that graph. The tailored cover letter is another output. The interview prep is another. The LinkedIn content strategy is another. When a user comes back to LinkRight after three months away, the system should know them better than they knew themselves when they left — because every interaction adds a node to the graph, every application outcome adds an edge, and every follow-up answer to a career highlight deepens the semantic weight of the relevant experiences. I built the foundation of this graph using a structure I call "career nuggets" — atomic, self-contained career facts with embeddings, importance scores, temporal metadata, and links to the source experience. Each nugget answers a question ("What is an example of cross-functional leadership you've demonstrated?"), and the answer is a paragraph-length, semantically rich, first-person account that can be retrieved, composed, and sent to exactly the right place. The career graph I envision isn't a visualization gimmick — it's a living, queryable representation of a person that gets smarter every week. That is the product I am building.

As of today, the platform is live at sync.linkright.in. The onboarding flow handles resume upload (PDF, DOCX, TXT), hybrid parsing, structured experience extraction, first-person narrative generation, and preference capture. The Scout job-matching system ranks live openings against the user's career memory. The Broadcast pillar supports LinkedIn OAuth, post scheduling via n8n, and engagement tracking. The dashboard surfaces daily ranked roles, profile growth metrics, and a Kanban-based applications tracker. The interview prep hub offers eight drill types. The daily diary captures voice-logged career moments via Whisper. Wave 2 of the design system — 21 screens designed in close collaboration between product thinking and AI-assisted design — is implemented. None of this existed eleven months ago. I built it by shipping something every day, learning from what broke, and staying honest with myself about whether I was building product or building infrastructure. The journey is not finished. But it has already taught me more about how products are actually made than any classroom could have.

## Story 4: Shipping LinkRight to PyPI — Distribution as a Product Skill

> *Use this for: "Tell me about a time you took something from prototype to production / a hard learning about engineering discipline / how you handle versioning and package distribution / a moment you bridged the PM-developer gap / a time you made a strategic 'do not do' decision"*

---

In early May 2026, a few weeks after the Wave 2 design ship and the CLI tool reaching A-grade resume quality (76.7 → 97.2 in one evening of iteration), I made a decision that on paper looked technical but in practice was a product decision: I would publish LinkRight to PyPI so that any human on the planet could install the CLI with `pip install linkright`. Up until that point, the tool lived inside `context/cli/linkright/` of my private repo. Anyone who wanted to use it had to git clone, navigate to the inner directory, run `pip install -e .`, then deal with whatever environment quirks their machine had. That works for me — I am the maintainer. It does not work for distribution. And distribution, I had been slow to learn, is the part of building a tool where it stops being a personal project and starts being a product.

The journey from "works on my machine" to "globally pip-installable" took a single session and taught me more about Python's packaging discipline than any tutorial ever had. The first thing I had to internalize is the difference between editable mode and installed mode. `pip install -e .` is the developer's view — it links the source tree directly into Python's site-packages, so any change you make is reflected immediately. The installed wheel is what real users get — only what you have explicitly declared in `pyproject.toml` is bundled. The two views look identical from the outside, but they differ on what they consider "part of the package." This is the silent gotcha that bit me almost immediately. The `harness/` directory at the root of the CLI dir — which contains the iteration loop, the scorecard logic, and seven other modules my pipeline depends on — was a sibling to `src/linkright/`, not inside it. In editable mode the source root is on `sys.path`, so `from harness.scorecard import Dimension` worked seamlessly. In installed mode, the source root is not on the path, and `harness/` was never in the wheel because my `pyproject.toml` only declared `where = ["src"]`. The first published version, 0.1.0, was structurally broken from the moment it landed on PyPI — installable but unimportable. The error surfaced the moment I tested in a fresh venv and ran `linkright tldr`: `ModuleNotFoundError: No module named 'harness'`. That was the first lesson I am carrying forward — never trust an editable install. Always test in a fresh venv before publishing, because that is the only environment that matches what your users will actually see.

The fix taught me the second discipline: PyPI is permanent. Once a version is published, that slot is consumed forever. You cannot re-upload `0.1.0` even with a fix. You can yank it — which marks it as broken so default `pip install` skips it — but you cannot delete or replace it. So I learned the version-bumping rhythm in real time. 0.1.0 went broken. I added `harness/` to the wheel via `where = ["src", "."]` and `include = ["linkright*", "harness*"]`, bumped to 0.1.1, rebuilt with `python -m build`, validated with `twine check`, and uploaded with the API token I had created at pypi.org/manage/account/token/. Tested in a fresh venv: `from linkright.jobsearch import evaluator, scorecard` worked. Imports clean. Smoke test passed. But then I noticed `linkright --version` was reporting `0.1.0` despite the package being 0.1.1 — and that revealed the third lesson: never have two sources of truth for a version string. My `pyproject.toml` said `0.1.1` (which is what PyPI saw), but `src/linkright/__init__.py` had a hardcoded `__version__ = "0.1.0"` that Click's `@version_option` was reading. Industry pattern is to use `from importlib.metadata import version; __version__ = version("linkright")` so the runtime always reads from the installed package metadata — single source of truth, zero drift forever. I applied that fix, bumped to 0.1.2, rebuilt, uploaded. Three published versions in a single afternoon. The first broken, the second functional with cosmetic drift, the third clean. That is not a flattering arc, but it is an honest one — and the muscle memory of "test in fresh venv before every publish, then verify --version reads from importlib.metadata" is now permanent.

Beyond packaging mechanics, this session forced me to make a real distribution-strategy decision. I had originally said I wanted to publish to both PyPI and npm because I wanted the broadest possible reach. The honest answer my AI collaborator pushed back with — and I had to think hard about — was that npm is anti-pattern for a Python CLI. The only way to publish to npm is to write a thin wrapper package whose `bin` script delegates to `pipx install linkright` on first run. That requires the user to have Python installed already, which means npm adds zero new users — it just adds a fragile indirection layer plus maintenance burden. The famous Python CLIs I respect — `httpie`, `aider-chat`, `ruff`, `black`, `poetry`, `pipx` itself — none of them ship to npm. They ship to PyPI primarily, and a Homebrew tap secondarily for the macOS users who do not want to think about Python. That is the pattern I chose. PyPI now (live), Homebrew tap later (one day's work, probably v0.2.0). It was a small but important strategic moment of saying "follow the canonical path, do not optimize for breadth on the wrong axis." That is a product decision, not an engineering one, and I learned to recognize it as such. The "what NOT to do" decisions are often more valuable than the "what to do" ones.

The CLI ergonomics work that paired with publishing was its own education. Earlier in the session I had built out twelve top-level commands (tailor, score, improve, critique, fill-metrics, practice, strategy-review, profile, etc.) and the user-facing surface was overwhelming. Real industry tools do not expose twelve verbose names — git has `ci` for `commit`, kubectl has unique-prefix matching where `kubectl get po` resolves to `pods`, npm has aliases for nearly every command. I implemented an `AliasedGroup` Click subclass that does three-tier resolution — exact name, then registered alias, then unique-prefix match — and registered short aliases at both the top-level (`linkright t` → `tailor`, `linkright f` → `fill-metrics`, `linkright crit` → `critique`) and the sub-group level (`linkright resume t` → `tailor`). The trick was that aliases at the top-level had to be registered via `add_alias()` rather than `add_command()`, because Click's `--help` walks `list_commands()` and would otherwise show every alias as a separate command, exploding a clean 12-command help into a noisy 38-entry table. I split the registration: canonical names go through `add_command` (visible in help), aliases through the AliasedGroup's internal `_aliases` dict (resolution-only, hidden from help). Then I added two new commands that mature CLIs always have: `linkright tldr` to print a curated cheat sheet for the most common workflow, and `linkright doctor` to run a nine-check health table covering config, profile, LLM keys, embedder, PDF renderer, parser, MongoDB, and agent backend. These primitives are the difference between "tool I would recommend" and "tool I would avoid." That is another skill I am carrying forward — the discipline of treating CLI surface as a product surface, not a developer convenience.

The throughline of this session, the thing I will remember six months from now when I forget the specific files I edited, is that I am no longer a PM who happens to be tinkering with a side project. I shipped a working Python package to a global registry, made it installable with one command in any developer's terminal anywhere in the world, and learned the full distribution stack — `pyproject.toml` schema (PEP 621), `extras_require` named groups, `setuptools.packages.find` with multi-`where` for src + companion-package layouts, `python -m build` mechanics, `twine` upload with API-token authentication, PyPI immutability rules, version-drift traps, CLI ergonomics design — in a single afternoon. I did not become a developer. I became a PM who can ship distribution. There is a difference. A developer focuses on the code. A distribution-aware PM focuses on the path the user takes from "I heard about this tool" to "I'm using it daily" — and that path runs through PyPI metadata, install commands, version semantics, the first sixty seconds of `linkright tldr`, and the choice of which registries to publish to versus which to deliberately skip. By the end of the session, when a fresh venv on any machine could `pip install linkright[full]` and have a working career-tailoring tool sixty seconds later, I understood viscerally what shipping means. It means the product exists outside of you. The hardest skill I built today was the patience to do the cleanup three times — broken, functional, clean — instead of pretending the first was good enough.
